# The interpretation guide

*How to read what the game shows you. It explains why; it never suggests
what.* (The E5 rule, which every surface in the product obeys — this
document included.)

## The reported/true toggle: two acts

**Act one — the marks.** Private assets do not get priced by a market every
month; they get appraised. Flip to "as true" and the smooth private lines
turn jagged: that is the same portfolio, seen honestly. The smoothness of
the reported plane is not a lie exactly — it is how the books everyone uses
actually behave — but it is a *delay*, and the delay always flatters the
recent past.

**Act two — coverage on both bases.** This is the act that costs money when
it is misread. Coverage is your undrawn commitments divided by what your
book is worth — the measure of how much you still owe against what you
have. The toggle changes the DENOMINATOR: on reported marks, a crash barely
dents your book for two quarters, so coverage looks calm exactly when the
true number is deteriorating. The book panel states both. When they
disagree, the gap is the appraisal delay at work, and every committee in
history has had to decide which number to believe. The arithmetic warning
in the post-game review exists for the sessions where the reported number
won the argument.

## Reading the outcome card

Alpha is your final value minus the policy twin's — same world, same
shocks, only the decisions differ. The twin is not passive: it rebalances
to plan, paces its commitments toward the policy weight on reported marks,
and gets forced to sell only when its own cash runs out. Beating it means
your deviations from policy added value; losing to it means they cost. The
drift twin (the third line on the analysis chart) never adjusts its pacing
at all — the space between the two twins is what disciplined pacing is
worth in that world, with nobody's judgement involved.

## Reading the review

Each line prices one window's decision given everything decided before it
and mechanical policy after it; the lines sum exactly to your alpha. Below
them, two things the numbers may have noticed:

- **The flinch cost** appears when you cut commitments. It re-runs your
  decade with only that cut restored to plan and states the distributions
  you gave up. Money you do not commit cannot come back.
- **The arithmetic warning** appears when a defensive move coincided with a
  coverage rise that was denominator-driven — your book fell, your
  obligations did not grow — and the move itself cost points. It names the
  mechanism, not the mistake.

## What none of this is

A simulated decade resampled from real months is an argument, not a
forecast. The platform's own evidence documents say plainly that nothing
here is decision-ready; the game teaches the shape of the trade-offs, and
the audit trail (the provenance panel) shows where every month came from.
Not investment advice.
