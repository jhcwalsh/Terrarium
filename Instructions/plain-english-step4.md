# Step 4 in plain English

*Companion to `KICKOFF-STEP4.md` and `STEP4-ARTIFACTS-ACTORS-PLAN.md`. This is
the explanation, not the authority — where they disagree, the plan wins.*

---

## What Step 4 is for

Steps 0–3 built a machine that produces numbers: simulated decades of markets,
funds that draw and return capital, an institution that lives through it all.
The numbers are honest, but nobody experiences a decade as a spreadsheet.
People experience it as *news*: a wire story about spreads blowing out, a
quarterly letter from a fund manager explaining a bad quarter, a board pack
landing two weeks before a decision.

Step 4 builds the storytellers — and then the first non-human decision-makers
to sit inside the world. Two very different kinds of AI, held to two very
different disciplines.

## The two disciplines

**The narrator must never invent a number.** Every artifact — wire item, data
release, manager letter, board pack — is generated *from the tape* (the
simulated data), and a gate checks it: every number must appear in the
artifact's approved claims table, every company and person must be fictional,
and nothing may know the future — an artifact dated March cannot mention
April. If a generated letter fails the gate, it gets two retries; then the
system falls back to a plain, template-built version. Boring but honest beats
fluent but wrong, every time.

**The actors must never leave their box.** Later in the step, AI committees
make allocation decisions inside the simulation. They choose only from a
bounded menu of allowed actions, see only what has been revealed so far (no
peeking at the future), file a written rationale for every decision, and are
benchmarked against human committees and against dumb-but-honest baselines
(simple rules, random-within-bounds, do-nothing). If the AI committee can't
beat or at least resemble the humans, that gets published too.

And one rule above both: **nothing generated by an AI ever touches the
certified numbers.** The narrator describes the tape; it cannot edit it. The
actors' decisions reshape their own institution's holdings; they cannot move
the market. A structural test enforces this the same way earlier steps
enforced that the engine can't read the narrative.

## The two tiers of storyteller

**Tier 1 is rules, not AI.** Most of what a reader sees — morning digests,
data-release pages, central bank statements, quarterly statements, the board
pack — is assembled by deterministic templates. Same world, same seed, same
words, every time. This is where the volume lives, and it costs nothing per
page. Thanks to Step 3, these templates now include the loud events: a
capital call arriving, a distribution landing, and the forced-sale notice,
which should read like the distress event it is.

**Tier 2 is AI with a leash.** Two artifact types — manager letters and
research notes — get real generated prose, because voice matters there. But
the prose is written *around* a payload built by deterministic code: the
numbers are computed first, frozen into a claims table, and the language
model is only allowed to describe them. The gate then checks the result
line by line.

## The World Bible

Before the AI writes anyone's letter, the world needs a cast: the fictional
institution, the fund managers (bound to Step 3's "hero funds," so a named GP
has real simulated numbers behind it), the research houses, the media
outlets. The World Bible is that cast list, machine-validated: names are
screened against real-company registries so no fictional GP accidentally
shares a name with a real one, and character arcs are checked against the
world's economics — a manager can't be scripted to have a great crisis if the
numbers say his fund got crushed.

## Playing it live

Step 4 also makes worlds *playable in calendar time*: the decade is
precomputed and sealed (a hash proves nobody rewrote history mid-game), then
revealed at wall-clock speed. Decision windows open on schedule — and, new in
this step, when events warrant: a spread breach or a collateral call opens an
unscheduled window, because real committees don't only meet quarterly.
Committees (human or AI) can also write down their intended rules at the
start — "if spreads pass 600 we do X" — and the system measures whether they
actually followed their own playbook when the moment came.

## What could go wrong, and the built-in answers

- *The AI computes a number instead of copying one.* The gate blocks derived
  arithmetic outright — if the number isn't in the claims table, the artifact
  fails.
- *A fake front page looks real enough to screenshot.* The watermark is
  applied in the renderer and re-applied on export, and there's a test for it.
- *The AI committee looks brilliant because it's inhumanly calm.* That
  "too-rational" tendency is one of the documented pathologies Step 4 must
  measure and publish internally — before any client-facing claim, not after.
- *Prompt tweaks quietly change everything.* Every prompt is versioned; any
  change re-runs a frozen ~30-scenario regression set; pass-rate and voice
  drift are reviewed.

## Where things stand at kickoff

Step 3 closed with an honest failure on record: the 2022 replay missed one
sealed criterion (hedge-fund marks bottomed too early). Step 4 inherits that
tape *as is* — the narrator will faithfully report numbers we know are
imperfect, because the alternative (quietly making the story read better than
the data) is exactly what this project is built to never do.

The three companion documents the plan requires are now all in hand — the
authoring rulebook (with the letter-gate's nine checks), the example World
Bible, and the schema. One honest wrinkle: the rulebook and example were
rebuilt from a session transcript after the originals were lost, and their
reconstruction notes say so plainly, listing the two small gaps and one known
inconsistency rather than papering over them. The rule that follows: anything
ambiguous in the rebuilt text gets asked about, not guessed at, and nothing
is frozen against it until the owner confirms it stands. The kickoff document
lists the remaining decisions for the owner, of which the real-company
screening list and the human-cohort question are the two with real lead time.
